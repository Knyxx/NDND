Once, Maggie fit between branches.

Now, the world seems small even while closing in around her.

The ground presses back when she lands. Her body is long and wrong, heavier than it should be, built for reach instead of lift. She still expects the sky to answer when she jumps, still feels the old certainty that air will rise to meet her—but it never does. The failure passes quickly. Lingering is useless.

Instinct moves her forward.

Shiny things matter.

They catch her eye and steady something restless inside her. Coins flash like beetle backs. Polished edges gleam like wet feathers after rain. She gathers quickly and quietly, before attention can turn her way, keeping brightness close where it feels safe.

Some sharp things matter more, she carries two.

They are light as feathers in her hands, balanced so perfectly they disappear until needed. She keeps them close to her arms, tucked where they do not catch the eye, the way talons once hid beneath plumage. She does not brandish them. She does not test their edge.

She strikes only when it is right.

Beneath the hunger for shine lies something heavier.

_Treasure._

The feeling has weight and certainty. The treasure belongs with her. This is not a thought. It is simply true.

Maggie moves through the world in quick bursts and sudden stillness. She pauses often, head tilting, listening to spaces others ignore. When startled, she does not thrash or flee—she tightens and waits. Her balance is perfect. Her body remembers how to be ready.

As night settles over an encampment, voices drift through the dark.

Gold. Stored away. Guarded.

Her attention snaps sharp. This pull is different—deeper, heavier. This treasure matters more than the others.

When the fires burn low, Maggie moves.

She slips through shadow and clutter, climbing where it feels right. Her hands and feet find holds without thought. The swords remain hidden, silent. She pauses, listens, darts again. The pull leads her to a wide, squat building at the edge of the camp—thick doors, tall walls, the smell of dust and old things.

Inside, the space opens wide.

Racks and crates rise toward the ceiling. Tables crowd together beneath them, covered in oddities: carved stone fragments etched with worn symbols, broken blades that hum faintly, glass lenses that bend light in unsettling ways. Bins hold metal limbs and jointed parts—arms, fingers, plates shaped like nothing alive.

Some things shine.

Others do not—but still feel important.

Maggie’s breath quickens. Her head turns again and again. Too much. Wonderful. She moves lightly, touching nothing she does not need to. The urge to gather claws at her, but she holds it down.

Not yet.

Then she sees it.

At the far end of the warehouse rests a giant golden sphere.

It is enormous—taller than she is wide. Its surface glows softly, deep and steady, not bright but heavy, as if the light has weight. The air around it lies still, pressed flat.

Maggie stops.

This is more than shiny.

She creeps closer, circling it, head tilting. Her reflection stretches and warps across its curved surface. Inside, something casts a shadow—a tall shape, unmoving, caught in the heart of the glow.

Her chest tightens.

Big. Tall. Important.

A single word brushes her thoughts, old and heavy, then slips away before she can hold it.

_Dragon._

The sphere shifts.

Just a little.

Maggie freezes.

Then it wobbles—and begins to roll.

Toward her.

Fear flutters sharp and fast. Her body chooses before her mind can.

_Up._

She jumps.

Arms spread, shoulders straining, certain the air will catch her. For one bright, foolish heartbeat, she believes it will.

It doesn’t.

She slams into a rack instead. Artifacts clatter and ring, scattering across the floor. She twists, drops, and retreats fast, steps light and quick, backing away without turning.

The wall meets her spine.

No more space.

The golden sphere rolls closer, filling her vision. The floor trembles beneath its weight. The glow deepens.

Maggie presses flat against the cold stone, breath coming fast.

The golden sphere crashes into her.
