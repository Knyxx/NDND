He was raised beneath wide skies and honest labour.

[[Hardacre]] lay low against the hills, its fences straight, its fields disciplined by season and sweat. Wind moved clean across the land, carrying the scents of turned earth, cut hay, and steady woodsmoke from a chimney that never failed. It was a small holding, but it stood firm, anchored as much by will as by timber and stone.

His father, [[Brukk Oxfell|Brukk]] embodied that firmness. Thick hands, scar-lined knuckles, a back that bent only when the work demanded it. He spoke little, endured much, and expected the same. His presence was not loud, yet it filled every room and furrow.

[[Thyra Oxfell|Thyra]], his mother’s strength ran quieter but no less deep. She read the weather in the bellies of clouds and the health of soil in its scent. She would kneel in the fields, crumble dirt between her fingers, lift it to her nose, and breathe in as though listening. Clay spoke differently than loam. Peat carried a darker note. She could taste rain in the air hours before it fell. The world spoke in wind and earth and growing things, and she answered by paying attention.

From her, Gar-ruk first learned that every place had a scent. A farm was not merely seen; it was known by the sharp sweetness of fresh hay, the mineral damp of morning soil, the faint sourness of livestock, the resin of split wood. To smell the dirt was to know whether it would yield or fail. To breathe deeply was to understand where one stood.

His brother Durough was more legend than memory. Gar-ruk knew him through stories told by firelight—campaign roads stretching endless and muddy, narrow escapes from ambush, a duel fought knee-deep in river water. When he left, he left behind his old armour, inherited from their grandfather: dented, worn, but meticulously kept. “To protect the little one,” their parents had said.

He would grin as he fastened it on, re-enacting his battles for an audience of two, often falling asleep still wrapped in leather, claiming he was “at his post.” Their parents would exchange looks of pride and quiet amusement. Even then, strength was never preached in that house. It was demonstrated.

At twelve winters, the wolves came.

They came wrong.

Not lean shapes testing the livestock pens. Not cautious hunters circling for weakness. These wolves struck at dusk with reckless fury, foam hanging from their jaws, eyes clouded and wild. They hurled themselves against the fences as if commanded rather than driven by hunger. Wood splintered. Iron shrieked. They reached the yard before the family fully rose from their beds.

His father met them with axe and torch.

Gar-ruk remembered the smell first. Wet fur. Hot pitch. Fresh-spilled blood, copper-thick in the throat. He remembered the tremor of the ground beneath pounding bodies. He remembered his mother’s hand locking around his wrist hard enough to bruise.

She understood before he did.

They could not all escape.

She did not scream. She dragged him toward the treeline, one hand pulling him, the other gripping a kitchen knife. Behind them, his father stepped into the breach and held it. The last image burned into Gar-ruk’s mind was a figure carved in firelight—broad shoulders unmoving as snapping jaws closed around him.

They did not stop running until the trees swallowed the farm whole.

Gar-ruk remained hidden in the brush through the long night and into the grey of morning. Smoke drifted through the branches. The sounds faded to nothing. When daylight strengthened, his mother left him concealed and walked back alone.

She returned hours later.

Her eyes were red. Her face hollowed by something deeper than exhaustion. She carried blankets, what little food she could gather, and a large sack. She did not speak of what she had seen. She took his hand and led him away without looking back.

They found shelter in a nearby village, lodging in a house that was itself trying to begin again. Two years passed in tight, careful survival. His mother worked where she could. Gar-ruk kept quiet, watched, and learned.

Then she fell ill.

It came sudden and violent. One week she worked a narrow strip of borrowed soil behind the lodge, strong and steady. The next she lay brittle and fevered, strength draining from her as if poured through unseen cracks. Her hands trembled. Her breath rasped. Her skin carried a faint bitter scent he did not understand.

She withered before his eyes.

With a final bloody cough, she fixed her gaze on him—not in confusion, but in fear—and then she was gone.

He knew nothing of healing. Nothing of herbs beyond planting them. Nothing of how to fight what could not be struck with steel.

All he knew was that he was alone.

And it was no longer safe to remain.

The road stripped softness from him quickly. Hunger sharpened him. Cold hardened him. He learned that survival depended less on strength than on attention.

Steel became his companion long before it became his craft.

Desperation drove him to attempt robbery once along a lonely stretch of road. The attempt ended before it began. Two blades crossed at his throat so swiftly he never saw them drawn.

The old master did not kill him.

Instead, he studied him, then allowed him to follow.

Under that man’s instruction, motion was reduced to purpose. Waste was beaten out of him—waste of breath, waste of effort, waste of thought.

He trained his senses before his hands.

He learned to know a place by scent before sight. Each village carried its own signature: tallow smoke and damp wool, fish rot and brine, coal ash and iron filings. Forests spoke differently—pine resin sharp in the air, wet loam heavy beneath it, the faint copper sweetness where predators had fed.

He knelt often, pressing fingers into soil, bringing them to his nose as his mother once had. Freshly disturbed earth smelled bright and raw. Long-settled ground held a deeper, settled note. He learned the musk of wolf from the musk of dog. He learned the metallic hint of rain building over stone. He learned the sour edge of fear rising from a man who meant to draw steel.

To survive was to listen with more than ears.

He refined his technique: two blades, never flourished, never wasted. Strike where it ended the fight. Leave nothing unnecessary behind—not in a footprint, not in a cut, not in a life taken. He moved with the wind so it masked his scent. He tracked by bent stems and crushed moss. He hunted without stirring crows from carrion.

He learned to kill without spectacle.

It made him capable. Dependable.

One day, it would make him great.

As his body hardened into scar and muscle, his memory sharpened as well. He replayed the night of the wolves—the once-sturdy gates knocked in almost cleanly, the coordinated assault. He remembered, too, the faint bitter scent that had lingered near his mother’s cup in her final days, a detail meaningless at the time.

Patterns formed.

Wolves do not break gates. Illness does not consume a healthy woman in days without cause.

His family had not been claimed by misfortune.

They had been targeted.

Somewhere in the wide world walked the only other blood of his line—if his brother still lived.

Gar-ruk turned toward the Crossroads Encampment, where trade routes converged and rumours travelled faster than caravans. Mercenaries passed through such places. So did secrets.

He arrived in late afternoon, dust clinging to his boots. Before entering, he paused and drew a slow breath.

Cooked fat. Horse sweat. Cheap ale. Oiled leather. Beneath it, heavier than it should have been—iron. Armed men in number.

He filed the knowledge away.

He bought a bowl of stew and ate without tasting it, listening instead. Talk of border contracts. A company shattered in the north.

Before nightfall, Gar-ruk climbed a quiet rise beyond the encampment’s outer ring. High ground. Clear sightlines. He pressed his fingers into the soil—dry, undisturbed. No recent passage. Satisfied, he began erecting his lean-to with mechanical efficiency.

Then the air shifted.

A sharp metallic tang cut through the evening breeze—hot stone, scorched iron, something electric beneath it.

The world convulsed.

An almighty crash split the twilight. The ground bucked beneath his boots. Birds erupted from the treeline in a frantic black cloud. Dust, splintered wood, and acrid smoke stung his eyes. His ears rang with the roar of shattered earth.

Gar-ruk froze.

Hand to hilt. Breath controlled. Every muscle coiled.

He tasted the air—sulphur, static, faint rich metal—and beneath it all, sharp and unnatural lingering taste.

He crested the rise.

A crater gouged the hillside, smoke curling from shattered stone and torn earth, leading up to a warehouse, it's side blown out, a path of destruction stretching downward to a broken cairn.

And somewhere in that chaos, the first answer waited.
